What to do when a debt collector contacts you about a collection account

A call or letter about a collection account can feel urgent. You do not have to decide on the spot whether the amount is right. Start by writing down who contacted you, what they said, and when. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) recommends keeping letters, copies, and notes of conversations.

Read it yourself: FDCPA validation rule, 15 U.S.C. § 1692g · CFPB validation-notice guide · FCRA dispute rule, 15 U.S.C. § 1681i.

Key takeaways

  • Look for the validation information and the date your 30-day dispute period ends. CFPB
  • If you dispute all or part of the debt in writing during that period, the collector must pause collection on the disputed amount until it responds as the rule requires. CFPB
  • A dispute with a collector is different from a dispute with a credit reporting company. Accurate negative information can remain on a report. CFPB dispute guide · CFPB reporting guide

First, check who is contacting you

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, or FDCPA, limits abusive, unfair, and deceptive conduct by covered debt collectors. It generally applies to collection of personal, family, or household debts by third-party collectors, debt buyers, and some lawyers. It generally does not cover the original creditor collecting its own debt under its own name. State rules may add protections. CFPB

Ask for the collector's name and contact details. Compare them with the notice and your own records. Do not send original documents; keep them and share copies if you decide to dispute an amount already paid. The CFPB recommends checking that you are dealing with a legitimate collector before sharing payment records.

Read the validation information

A covered collector generally must provide validation information in the first communication or within five days, subject to the rule's exceptions. The notice generally identifies the collector, the current creditor, the account number if any, an itemized amount, the current amount, and how to respond. It should show the end date of the validation period. CFPB regulation · CFPB guide

Compare the creditor name, amount, fees, payments, and account details with your statements. If something seems wrong, note the exact difference. The notice is a starting point for checking the claim, not a command to accept it. Under the FDCPA, failing to dispute during the notice period does not mean you legally admit owing the debt.

Use the 30-day written dispute window

The validation period generally runs for 30 days after you receive, or are assumed to receive, the validation information. The notice should identify its end date. To invoke the collection-pause rule, send a written dispute of the debt or the disputed part during that period. You can also request the original creditor's name and address in writing if it differs from the current creditor. CFPB regulation · FDCPA

Say which account you mean and what you question. For example, identify a payment missing from the balance or explain that you do not recognize the account. Ask for verification. You may keep a copy and proof of sending; the CFPB suggests certified mail as one way to document delivery. Avoid sending original records.

If a timely written dispute is received, the collector must stop collecting the debt or disputed portion until it sends verification or a copy of a judgment, subject to the regulation's rule for duplicative disputes. A timely written request for original-creditor information also pauses collection until the response described by the rule. A pause is not cancellation of the debt; collection may resume after the required response. CFPB rule · CFPB explanation

You can write after the period ends, but the special pause tied to a timely written dispute may not apply. If you receive court papers, do not assume a validation letter replaces a response to the court; seek local legal help about the specific deadline. The CFPB notes that timing affects rights, and the FDCPA treats formal pleadings differently from an initial collection communication.

Check what your credit report actually says

A credit report can show account balances, payment history, creditors, and collection items. It may not look like the collector's notice, so compare account details rather than assuming both records say the same thing. CFPB credit-report guide

You can get a free report from each of the three nationwide reporting companies once a week through AnnualCreditReport.com, according to the CFPB's current guide. Check the creditor, balance, dates, and whether the account is yours. Save the page that contains the item so you can point to a specific error. If an item is accurate, a collection letter alone will not make it disappear. Most accurate negative information may lawfully remain for up to seven years. CFPB

Dispute a reporting error separately

If your report has a wrong balance, an account that is not yours, or incomplete details, dispute the specific issue with the credit reporting company. The CFPB recommends explaining what is wrong and why, and sending copies of supporting records. It also recommends disputing with the business that supplied the information. Keep your originals.

Under FCRA section 611(a)(5), 15 U.S.C. § 1681i, after reinvestigation, an item found to be inaccurate or incomplete or that cannot be verified must be promptly deleted or modified as appropriate. This is not a promise that every disputed item will be deleted. Section 611(a)(3) also allows a credit reporting company to treat a dispute as frivolous or irrelevant in some cases, including when there is too little information to investigate. Specific facts and supporting details help it understand your claim. Statute · CFPB steps

Make your first dispute simple, specific and well documented

Do not spend hours trying to make your first credit-report dispute sound like a legal brief. Print the report page and circle or highlight each item you believe is inaccurate or incomplete. For each item, say in your own words what is wrong, why you think so, and what you want corrected. Send a copy of that marked page and copies, not originals, of supporting records. A reporting company may reasonably find a dispute frivolous or irrelevant if you give too little information to investigate, so details matter. CFPB dispute guide · 15 U.S.C. § 1681i(a)(3)

Save your letter, what you enclosed, proof of mailing or online submission, and every response. Scan or photograph paper letters and keep dated PDFs or photos on a protected phone or computer; when sharing records outside the bureau or information provider's dispute process, black out SSN and account numbers that are not needed. If a response is delayed or missing, your file can show exactly what you sent and when, for a follow-up, a lawyer or an eligible CFPB complaint. You can seek legal help at any time. Three common questions

Does asking for validation erase a collection account? No. A timely written dispute triggers a temporary pause in collection until the collector responds as required; it does not itself erase accurate credit-report information. CFPB collection rule · CFPB reporting guide

What if I already paid? Tell the collector what is wrong, and, after checking who contacted you, send copies of payment records rather than originals. CFPB

Must I dispute every bureau separately? Send a dispute to each reporting company whose report contains the error, and consider disputing with the information provider too. CFPB

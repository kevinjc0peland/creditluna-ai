If your debt was sold: what to look for in the paperwork

A creditor can sell a consumer debt to a buyer; that buyer may later sell it again. A sale does not make a debt unreal or require a new agreement with each buyer. When someone collects or sues, ask who is claiming the right to collect this particular account, and what connects the account to that claimant? The CFPB explains that debts can be sold. Whether a collector or plaintiff must show a particular document depends on the setting and applicable law.

Read it yourself: FTC's 2013 debt-buying report and full PDF · Colorado's ownership-document rule · Illinois appellate opinion · 15 U.S.C. § 1692g.

Key takeaways

  • Read the sale document with its referenced portfolio list or account schedule. A generic bill of sale naming a portfolio may not, by itself, tell you whether your account was included.
  • Trace every named transfer from the original creditor to the person or business now claiming the right to collect. A collector acting for an owner and a buyer claiming ownership are not necessarily the same thing.
  • Do not treat an “as is” clause, an absent page, or a redaction as proof that a debt is false. Identify the actual gap and ask a precise question; a court applies its own evidence and procedure rules.

Why sale paperwork can be thin

In its 2013 study, the FTC reviewed large debt buyers' purchase practices, not every transaction today. It found that, in many agreements, sellers disclaimed warranties about the accuracy of individual-account information and sold debts, with some exceptions, “as is.” Buyers often received electronic account data but limited account documents at purchase; contracts also restricted how long or how often buyers could request documents afterward. The FTC's summary describes both the warranty and later-access limits.

The FTC did not directly test the accuracy of the transferred data. Its report says the study “did not test the accuracy of the information conveyed by debt sellers to debt buyers” and therefore could not conclude how prevalent errors were in debts sold “as is.” FTC report, p. 25. A limited warranty is a reason to check the underlying facts, not evidence that your own balance or account is wrong.

Read the bill of sale together with its attachments

Start with the seller, buyer, effective date and portfolio name. Compare the buyer named there with the current claimant. If a document says it transfers the accounts “listed on Schedule A,” is that schedule attached, and what does it show? Look for an account-level connection: an original creditor's account number (possibly only the last four digits), a debtor name or another usable identifier that you can compare with your records. An identifier can appear in an incorporated list rather than on the face of the bill of sale. Compare the claimed balance and dates with statements or payments you saved; the sale itself is not a complete account history.

For multiple transfers, write out the chain: original creditor → first buyer → next buyer → current claimant. Check that the seller in each later document is the buyer in the preceding one, and ask whether each transfer covers the same account. A missing or mismatched link is a question, not a self-executing victory. Our fictional annotated example shows what to notice, not what any court must accept.

Read the warranty language too. Does the seller promise the account data is accurate, disclaim that promise, or allow limited returns of accounts that fail specified criteria? Those are contract terms between seller and buyer, not a consumer's waiver of rights or automatic proof of an error. The FTC findings are a reason to check the sale-file details. FTC report

Redactions and proof are different questions

Paperwork may hide other people's account details or confidential pricing. Do not assume a blacked-out page is fake or that you must receive an entirely unredacted bill of sale. Ask instead: Can I still see how my specific account was included, and can the decision-maker see whatever the applicable rules require? If a referenced schedule is missing, say which one and why it matters. Courts may review confidential material.

For example, the 2024 Colorado Revised Statutes § 5-16-111(2)(b) says that, for a legal action on a debt owned by a debt buyer, an ownership writing must be attached, and if the debt was assigned more than once, “each assignment or other writing evidencing transfer of ownership must be attached to establish an unbroken chain of ownership”. This is a Colorado lawsuit rule, not a rule for every collection notice or every state.

The Illinois appellate court in Unifund CCR Partners v. Shah, 2013 IL App (1st) 113658, ¶¶ 14, 20–23, distinguished an outright sale from an assignment for collection under the Illinois law then at issue. On the incomplete appellate record, redacted incorporated schedules did not show the account information needed for the relevant collection assignments; the court explained, “While it is entirely possible that the unredacted versions satisfy section 8b, we cannot know because they have not been included in the record.” That was a case-specific ruling about the record and statute, not a rule that every redaction invalidates a sale. These examples do not tell you what your state or court requires; ask local counsel.

If you are contacted, or served with a lawsuit

Check the collector's validation notice for the current creditor, claimed amount and dispute instructions. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1692g, a timely written dispute of all or part of a debt generally pauses collection of the disputed amount until the covered collector sends the verification or judgment copy required by the statute. A timely written request for the original creditor's name and address, if different, has its own response rule. You can ask specific questions about ownership and account identification, but § 1692g does not automatically entitle everyone to a complete unredacted chain-of-title packet. The CFPB recommends keeping copies and a dated record.

If you are served, do not ignore the summons. Respond as the court papers require and seek a licensed attorney or legal aid promptly; a collector's validation letter is not a court answer. The CFPB warns that failing to respond can lead to a default judgment. For covered debt-collector lawsuits, 15 U.S.C. § 1692i generally specifies the judicial district where you signed the contract sued upon or resided when the suit began; it has a separate real-property rule. Do not assume an unfamiliar address alone proves improper venue. Ask a local attorney about the court and facts.

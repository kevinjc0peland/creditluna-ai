Every state’s deadline. How long can a debt collector come after you?

Here is a free one, and it is one of the most powerful things most people have never been handed. Ask somebody how long a debt collector can legally come after them, and they will shrug. Ask them about their OWN state, and they will shrug again. But the answer changes at the state line. In Virginia an open account goes quiet after 3 years (Va. Code 8.01-246). In Rhode Island it is 10. In California a payment does not bring a dead debt back at all. Every state is different, and today all of them go in one place.

Rule one. The state that can sue you sets the clock. Not the state named in your agreement. A collector cannot import another state's deadline by putting it in the fine print. That is settled case law. Portfolio Recovery Associates, LLC v. King, 14 N.Y.3d 410 (2010).

Rule two. The clock starts at the first missed payment. And a payment or a written acknowledgment usually restarts it. A growing list of states bars that restart on consumer debt outright. Watch the Watch column below.

Rule three. Credit cards are the fight. Some states count them as written contracts, some as open accounts, a few have never settled it. Where a collector cannot produce the cardholder agreement, the shorter period usually wins.

Why this matters against a collector. A debt past your state's deadline is time-barred. The collector can still call and still report, but they cannot sue you or threaten to sue you over it. A collector that threatens suit on a time-barred debt is making a false representation (15 USC 1692e(2)(J)), and federal rule 12 CFR 1006.26 puts that in writing for debt collectors. Know your state's number and you know when the threat is empty.

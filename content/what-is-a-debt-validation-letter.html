What is a debt validation letter, and what should it ask for?

A debt validation letter is your written reply when a covered debt collector says you owe a debt and you need to check it. It can dispute all or part of the claim, request the original creditor's name and address, or both. The law gives special protection to a written dispute or request made during the validation period. 15 U.S.C. § 1692g · CFPB rule

Read it yourself: 15 U.S.C. § 1692g · CFPB validation-notice regulation · CFPB dispute and original-creditor regulation.

Key takeaways

  • The collector's validation notice and your validation letter are different documents. CFPB · FDCPA
  • A written dispute sent during the validation period pauses collection of the disputed debt or portion until the collector responds as required. CFPB
  • The statute calls for verification or a judgment copy, not an automatic full contract or every statement. 15 U.S.C. § 1692g

First, understand the notice you received

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) governs covered debt collectors. It generally covers third-party collection of debts for personal, family, or household purposes, rather than an original creditor collecting its own debt under its own name. CFPB overview

Under 15 U.S.C. § 1692g(a), the collector generally must send written notice within five days after the first contact unless that information was already provided or the debt was paid. The CFPB's rule allows specified ways to deliver validation information at first contact or afterward. The notice generally includes the current creditor, amount, account details if available, itemization, and the date the 30-day period ends. CFPB regulation · CFPB plain-language guide

This is their notice. Your letter is your response. Writing a letter is useful when you have a real question about the debt, creditor, amount, or payments. First read the notice and compare it with records you already have. Write down the exact point you want checked. CFPB guide

What does the 30-day period mean?

The validation period begins when the collector provides validation information and generally ends 30 days after you receive it or are assumed to receive it. The notice should show the end date. A written dispute within that period makes the collector stop collecting the disputed debt or portion until it sends the required response. A timely written request for the original creditor's name and address pauses collection until that information is sent, or until the rule's same-creditor response is given. CFPB § 1006.34 · CFPB § 1006.38

A missed window does not turn silence into a legal admission that you owe the debt. You may still write to raise a concern, but do not assume you get the same collection-pause protection from an untimely letter. A debt collector may resume collection after sending the response the law requires. 15 U.S.C. § 1692g(c) · CFPB answer

What to put in your letter

Start with your name, mailing address, the collector's name and dispute address, the date, and enough account detail to identify the claim. The collector's notice should give the address where it accepts disputes and requests for original-creditor information. CFPB § 1006.34

State plainly whether you dispute the whole debt or a part. If you dispute only the amount, identify the part and why: a payment you made, a fee you question, or a creditor you cannot identify. Ask for verification of the disputed debt. If useful, ask for the original creditor's name and address when different from the current creditor. Those requests track § 1692g(a)(4)-(5) and (b).

Keep your words factual. You can say, for example, “I dispute the stated balance because my payment record shows a payment that I cannot find in your itemization.” Attach copies, not originals, of the records that support your point. If you already paid, the CFPB recommends checking that the collector is legitimate before sharing your proof. You do not need to adopt a dramatic script or state facts you do not know.

Sign and date a copy for your records. The CFPB suggests retaining a copy and using certified mail, with a return receipt if you want proof of delivery. Keep the validation notice and any reply together. These are practical recordkeeping steps, not a promise of any outcome. CFPB

What verification does, and does not, require

The FDCPA says the collector must obtain verification of the debt or a copy of a judgment and send a copy in response to a timely written dispute before continuing collection. For a timely original-creditor request, it requires the original creditor's name and address if different from the current creditor. The CFPB regulation allows the required response in writing or electronically under its delivery rules and addresses duplicative disputes. 15 U.S.C. § 1692g

You may ask for more detail to help you understand the claim, but do not tell yourself the federal statute always requires a signed original contract, a full payment history, or a special “wet-ink” document. Those things are not listed in § 1692g as universal requirements. Statute · CFPB rule

A validation letter does not by itself remove an account from a credit report. If your report has an error, separately dispute it with the reporting company and the provider of the information. Under FCRA section 611(a)(5), 15 U.S.C. § 1681i, an item found after reinvestigation to be inaccurate or incomplete or that cannot be verified must be promptly deleted or modified as appropriate. Under section 611(a)(3), a bureau may find a dispute frivolous or irrelevant in some cases, including if it lacks enough information. Give specific reasons and supporting details. Statute · CFPB guide

Accurate negative information can lawfully stay on a report, generally for up to seven years. No letter guarantees deletion. CFPB

If you also see an error on a credit report, make that a separate dispute with each reporting company that shows it; this is not the collector's validation process. For a first report dispute, print and circle the exact item, explain the error in your own words, and send copies of supporting records. Save dated letters, proof of sending and every response so you can follow up with facts; keep digital copies securely. See Make your first dispute simple, specific and well documented. Three common questions

Is a phone call enough for the pause? The special collection pause under § 1692g(b) depends on a timely written dispute or original-creditor request. Statute · CFPB rule

Can I ask about just one fee? Yes. You can dispute all or any portion of the debt, and the pause applies to the disputed portion. 15 U.S.C. § 1692g · CFPB rule

What if I never got validation information? Ask the collector for it and review the CFPB notice requirements. If the issue remains, the CFPB says you can submit a complaint. Do not guess when your period ended.
